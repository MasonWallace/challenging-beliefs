Facsimile 1: 'Abraham on the altar' — or an ordinary Egyptian funeral scene?

What Joseph Smith said

The Book of Abraham prints three Egyptian drawings with his explanations. In the first one, he said the man lying down is Abraham, tied to an altar. He said the man standing over him is a pagan priest about to kill him.

What the drawing actually is

Egyptologists recognise this scene right away. It is a funeral picture. The dog-headed god Anubis is preparing a body for burial. Hundreds of the same scene survive. This one was made for an Egyptian priest named Hor, about 1,500 years after Abraham lived.

The catch

There is a hole in the papyrus. It sits right where the priest's head and knife are shown in the printed version. So we cannot see the original for those two things. An artist drew them in for the 1842 printing.

How sure are we?

Fairly, but not completely. Scholars point to faint ink marks of a headdress, which fits a dog head, not a bald man. And no other picture like this has a knife in it. But because the hole covers the exact spot, we grade this one contested rather than settled.

Why it matters

The rest of the labels have no hole to hide behind. He called four jars the gods of foreign nations, and a crocodile the god of Pharaoh. Egyptologists say those readings are simply wrong. The church now admits its scholars read these drawings differently than he did.

What to say

"Can we look at what the Egyptologists say about the parts that aren't damaged?"

Read the full case — sources, the apologists' reply, and the Egyptology
The claim

Joseph Smith published Facsimile 1 as a picture of Abraham. He labelled the man on the couch as Abraham "fastened upon an altar," and the standing figure as "the idolatrous priest of Elkenah" about to kill him. Egyptologists identify the same drawing at a glance. It is a lion-couch vignette — a standard funeral scene showing the jackal-headed god Anubis tending the mummy of Osiris.

The original survives as Papyrus Joseph Smith I, part of a Book of Breathings made for a Theban priest named Hor around the second century BC, roughly 1,500 years after Abraham lived. There is one complication, and it is the heart of the argument. The papyrus has a hole in it, and the hole sits exactly where the priest's head and knife appear in the 1842 printed version. That printed version was drawn by a woodcut artist, not preserved on the papyrus.

Robert Ritner and other Egyptologists point to ink traces of a headdress at the shoulder, which fits a jackal head rather than a bald human one. No other lion-couch scene anywhere shows a knife. Smith's other labels on the same drawing fare worse: he calls the bird the Angel of the Lord, the four canopic jars the idolatrous gods of foreign nations, and the crocodile the idolatrous god of Pharaoh. None of those match any accepted Egyptological reading.

The defense, steelmanned

Egyptologist John Gee, Kerry Muhlestein and Pearl of Great Price Central make four arguments. First, the hole means nobody knows what was there. Some argue the papyrus was already torn in 1835. A knife and a human head could have filled the gap. They also note that this scene is genuinely unusual. The figure on the couch looks clothed and alive, and may have both hands raised.

Second, Egyptian temple art does show enemies being ritually killed. So reading a sacrifice here is not absurd on its face. Third, Jews living in Egypt reused Egyptian art and gave it new meaning. Abraham's name even shows up in later Egyptian magic texts. On that view the drawing could have illustrated an Abraham story whatever it was made for. Fourth, some argue the Book of Abraham was revealed rather than translated. The papyri would then be only a prompt. If that is right, the explanations are inspired commentary, not Egyptology, and judging them as Egyptology misses the point.

Source: Pearl of Great Price Central: The Idolatrous Priest (Facsimile 1, Figure 3)

Verdict — contested

Facsimile 1 is different from the other two, and that is why it is graded contested. Here there is a real gap in the evidence. The hole covers exactly the disputed parts. So the argument runs on parallels and faint ink marks rather than on a surviving original.

The scholarly reading is still far better supported. There are headdress traces at the shoulder. There are hundreds of parallel funeral scenes. There is not one that shows a knife. The church's own Gospel Topics essay admits that Egyptologists read the facsimiles differently than Joseph Smith did. But the defenders are arguing about what filled the hole, not about the Egyptology itself. And the scene does have odd features. That is enough to keep this figure at contested rather than unrefuted.

One thing to add: the other labels have no hole to hide behind. The four jars as foreign gods, the crocodile as Pharaoh's god — those readings have no support at all.
