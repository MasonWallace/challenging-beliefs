All Claims — ten more ways

The problem, in the owner's words: the flat list of every case is just too much — the viewer sees a bunch of cases and gets overwhelmed. And the four verdict words (UNANSWERED · ARGUABLE · ADMITTED · ANSWERED) don't make sense and bring in more confusion.

How to read this page. Ten different answers to one question: how does a person find the one case they need out of 119 — or 19? Each mockup is built with real titles and real counts, deliberately truncated (a few rows per group; the full page would show everything). Different mockups use different sections on purpose, to prove the ideas survive both the 119-case section and the 19-case one. Read each for its organising principle, not its pixels — and read the cost line under each, because every idea here has one.

First: the words that replace the verdict words

Used throughout the ten mockups below. Each verdict becomes a short phrase that tells the reader what to do, backed by one sentence wherever it appears as a group heading — the thing that made Option B work. One catch discovered in the data: the old words flip meaning between sections (in Mormonism, Answered means they have the answer — skip it; in Is There a God, Answered means the objection against Christianity is answered — good news). So these phrases must be per-section strings in the data, not one global rename. The Mormonism set:

The through-line. Every mockup keeps the strength axis in some form — sentence-headed bands (2, 10), plain chips (1, 3, 4, 7, 8, 9), a column position (5), or a notch meter (6) — because it is the page's most useful signal. What changes is whether strength is the primary sort (2, 5, 6, 10) or a passenger on a different organising principle: topic (1), the conversation (3, 7), pastoral cost (4), memory budget (8), or study order (9). None of the ten drops it.
